Dragon bone 龍骨車 lónggǔchē · 翻車 fānchē, "the overturning cart"

A jointed chain of square pallets in an inclined trough. No pots, no valves, no gearing. Each pallet nearly fills the section and pushes a pocket of water ahead of it up the slope. It trades lift for volume and for the fact that a farmer can carve a replacement pallet with a hand tool — which is why it stayed in service into the twentieth century.

Three things to find

The slope optimum. Watch the pocket, not the readout: its surface stays level while the trough tilts away beneath it, so every degree of slope thins the wedge each pallet carries. Steeper buys lift and costs volume. Somewhere in between, the water actually delivered — height times flow — is greatest. Nothing is labelled.

Pitch and slope are one decision. A long pitch at a steep angle spills over each pallet before the next arrives. Set the pitch to 40 cm and walk θ up: the pump dies around 25°. At 15 cm it survives the whole range. The two sliders cannot be set independently, and no drawing of the machine tells you that.

The speed window. Too slow and leaked water falls back past the pallet behind. Too fast and the pallets entrain air instead of filling. Change power and clearance and watch ηv.

And one thing to break

Push the trough past about four metres of lift, or drag clearance to "derelict", and leakage overwhelms delivery. Flow collapses and the water runs back down the trough. That one interaction answers "why not use this on a well" better than a paragraph can.
